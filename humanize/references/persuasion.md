# Persuasion integrity

Read this when the text is trying to get someone to do something: landing pages, sales emails, pitch decks, cold outreach, fundraising copy, job ads.

Humanizing persuasive copy creates a specific risk. "Make it more compelling" and "make it sound human" get blurred together, and the fastest way to make copy compelling is to add a claim. Under the no-invention rule, you can't — and the pressure to is highest exactly here.

## The rule, restated for this context

You may sharpen an existing claim. You may not create one, strengthen one beyond what the source supports, or remove a qualifier that limits one.

Specifically forbidden even when it would improve the copy:

- Numbers, percentages, or results not in the source
- Testimonials, customer names, logos, or quotes
- Deadlines, countdowns, seat counts, or inventory limits
- "Join 10,000+ teams" style social proof
- Credentials, awards, certifications, or press mentions
- Removing a hedge: "may reduce costs" → "reduces costs" is a fabrication, not an edit

## Triggers you can use, and how

Each of these works only when the underlying claim is true and the reader keeps a real choice.

| Trigger | Legitimate use | The version to refuse |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reciprocity | Lead with something genuinely useful | "Free" bait with a hidden obligation |
| Consistency | Reflect the reader's own stated goal, suggest a small next step | Escalation traps |
| Social proof | Real testimonials, real counts, comparable customers | Invented or vague "everyone's switching" |
| Authority | Named credentials, sources, method, limitations | Authoritative tone substituting for evidence |
| Liking | Match the reader's language and work toward their outcome | Flattery, mirroring, simulated intimacy |
| Scarcity | Real deadlines, real capacity limits | Fake timers, manufactured urgency |
| Unity | Authentic shared mission | Us-vs-them, identity pressure |
| Framing | Benefit *and* trade-off | Positive-only framing that hides cost |
| Loss aversion | Real risks of inaction, stated plainly | Fear-mongering |

## Non-manipulation gate

Before delivering persuasive copy, check:

- Every factual claim traces to the source
- Urgency and scarcity are real, or absent
- Trade-offs and costs aren't hidden
- The reader can decline easily — opt-out, pricing, and limitations are findable
- No guilt, fear, or pressure that wasn't in the original
- If the copy involves an AI product or an AI-written interaction, it doesn't misrepresent what's automated

A failure here means revise, regardless of how good the prose scores.

## When the source is thin

Often the copy is vague because the product story is vague. That's not yours to fix by invention. Say so:

> "The headline promises 'faster workflows' but nothing on the page says how much faster or than what. If you have a benchmark or a customer number, that single detail would do more than any rewrite. Without it I've kept the claim general rather than sharpening it into something the source doesn't support."

That's a more valuable deliverable than polished fiction.
