# Format adapters

Applying one "human voice" to every format is itself a tell. A Reddit comment and a compliance memo are both written by humans and share almost nothing.

Identify the target format before the rhythm pass. If the user hasn't said, infer from the file (slide deck, email thread, README) and state your assumption in the "What changed" note so they can correct it.

## Long-form article / blog post

Vary paragraph length hard — one-sentence paragraphs next to six-sentence ones. Subheads should name the content, not tease it ("How pricing works", not "The surprising truth about pricing"). Openers that summarize the whole piece before delivering it are the most common AI tell in this format; start with the actual first point. Contractions on. Let some paragraphs end flat.

## LinkedIn

The native failure mode is different from generic AI slop — it's the one-line-per-paragraph inspirational cadence with a rhetorical hook and a question at the end. Humanizing a LinkedIn post usually means *reducing* the performance: fewer line breaks, no hook-then-pause, no "Agree?" closer. Keep it under about 200 words. One concrete claim beats three abstract ones.

## X / Twitter

Compression is the whole format, so normal rhythm variation doesn't apply. Cut every word that isn't load-bearing. No wind-up. Threads: each post should stand alone; don't write connective tissue like "But here's the thing (2/7)". Avoid the numbered-lesson format unless the user asked for it.

## Reddit / forums

Genuinely conversational. Contractions, sentence fragments, parentheticals, mid-post self-correction. Stating uncertainty is normal and raises credibility here. Marketing register gets punished harder than bad grammar. No headers, no bullet-point structure unless the question calls for it.

## Email

Match the relationship. Cold outreach: short, specific, one ask, no flattery opener. Internal: drop the pleasantries scaffolding, lead with the ask or the decision. Reply: answer the question in the first line, context after. Cut "I hope this finds you well" and "Just circling back" without replacement.

Subject lines should describe, not tease.

## Documentation / technical writing

Restraint. The prose wrapper gets humanized; every spec, parameter, and code sample stays untouched. Don't add personality to reference material — a developer scanning for a parameter does not want voice. Concept and getting-started pages can carry more of it. Second person is standard here and isn't narrator distance.

Formal precision reads as AI to detectors. Ignore that. Do not degrade accuracy for tone.

## Landing pages / marketing copy

The highest fabrication risk in the whole skill. Every benefit claim, metric, testimonial, and scarcity signal must already exist in the source. Read `persuasion.md` before editing.

Typical repair: replace abstract benefit language ("streamline your workflow") with the concrete capability already described further down the page. Headlines should say what the thing does.

## Slide decks

Slides aren't prose. Humanizing usually means cutting to fragments, not building sentences. Speaker notes are where voice lives — those get the full treatment. Respect the character budget; a "more natural" line that overflows the text box is a regression.

## Scripts / video / voiceover

Written to be spoken. Read it aloud mentally: anything you'd stumble over gets rewritten. Contractions always. Sentence fragments fine. Long subordinate clauses don't survive speech. Keep the user's filler-word habits if their existing scripts have them — that's their delivery, not slop.

## Academic / research

Minimal intervention. Conventions that look like AI tells (passive voice, hedged claims, formal register, nominalizations) are often required by the field. Limit edits to genuine filler, redundancy, and tangled syntax. When in doubt, leave it and note it.

## Legal / compliance / policy

Prose wrapper only. Defined terms, obligations, conditions, and disclaimers stay verbatim. If humanizing a sentence could change its legal meaning, don't — flag it instead.
